2 of 10

Before accounting for anything about the schools, neighborhood location already explains 11–21% of score variation.

We asked: if you knew only which neighborhood a school is in — not its teachers, not its students, not its resources — how much of the score difference between schools could you predict?

11.1%Algebra — explained by location alone
21.4%ELA — explained by location alone
21.2%Living Environment — explained by location alone

For English, 21 out of every 100 points of difference between schools comes from location — before we know a single fact about the school itself.

These numbers — called the Intraclass Correlation Coefficient (ICC) — confirm that neighborhood membership explains a meaningful share of between-school score variation, enough to warrant a two-level statistical model.

3 of 10

School-level factors explain most of the between-neighborhood score gap — but not all of it. For ELA, 21% remains.

We added everything measurable about the schools: what students they serve (race, poverty, English learner status), average class size, and school type. For most exams, that's enough.

97.1%Algebra — school factors explain
78.8%ELA — school factors explain
95.2%Living Environment — school factors explain

For Algebra: once you know the school, the neighborhood adds almost nothing. For ELA: 21% of the between-neighborhood gap is still unexplained after accounting for everything about the school.

The remaining 21% of between-neighborhood ELA variance — unexplained by school-level factors — is what the NEQ policy context index is designed to test.

4 of 10

We built a score for each neighborhood based on its zoning — how the city has decided land can be used there.

We called it the NEQ (Neighborhood Educational Quality) index. It's built entirely from NYC zoning records — city planning data. No test scores go into building it.

Two ingredients

Commercial densityHow much commercial building the city allows — higher means more businesses, more mixed-use activity
Residential zoningWhat share of land is zoned residential-only — more residential means less mixed

The chart shows commercial density across the top 18 districts. Lower Manhattan is at 12.24. Fresh Meadows is at 0.002. The city set those numbers through zoning law.

This isn't about whether a neighborhood is rich or poor. It's about how the city has legally defined what can exist there.

7 of 10

The neighborhood association appears for English and science, but not for Algebra — and that subject-specificity is the central finding.

We ran the same analysis for all three Regents exams. The results split cleanly by subject.

Algebra+0.275 · p=.368 · no effect
English+1.180 · p=.003 · ★★ significant
Science+0.931 · p=.004 · ★★ significant

Math seems to live mostly inside the classroom. English and science seem to extend beyond the school into the wider community: the language students hear, the resources around them.

The Algebra null result is consistent with theory: mathematics instruction is more tightly coupled to in-school factors than language arts or science, which extend into the surrounding community environment.

Every predictor in the model, with effect sizes and confidence intervals, shown across all three exams.

Each row is one factor we measured. Each dot is its effect on scores. The horizontal bar shows our uncertainty. When a bar doesn't cross the center line, the effect is real and not just noise.

Three things worth noticing

English Language Learner % barely moves Algebra scores but it's the single biggest factor for ELA. ELA is a language test — students still learning English score lower on it.

Poverty % is negative for all three exams, consistently. The most reliable finding in the whole model.

The NEQ neighborhood score is highlighted at the bottom. Its bar clears zero for English and science. For Algebra, it overlaps zero — no clear effect.

10 of 10

Neighborhood zoning is associated with ELA and science performance after school-level controls, but not with Algebra.

This study looked at 409 NYC public high schools across 43 neighborhoods and asked: does the zoning character of a neighborhood predict how its schools perform, after accounting for everything measurable about the schools?

What we found

ELA ★★+1.18 points · p=.003 · holds across years
Science ★★+0.93 points · p=.004 · holds across years
Algebra+0.28 points · p=.368 · no effect

What this isn't: we're not saying rezoning a neighborhood would raise test scores. We're saying neighborhoods where the city allows more commercial and mixed-use activity tend to have higher ELA and science scores — even after school-level differences are accounted for.

The next question

Does the neighborhood effect hit some students harder than others? Does high poverty matter less in a better-zoned neighborhood? That's what this study opens up.
